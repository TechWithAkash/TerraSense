# Soil organic carbon, water holding capacity, and soil biota

Source excerpts: Hudson 1994 (Journal of Soil and Water Conservation); Lal 2004 (Science); Poeplau and
Don 2015 (Agriculture, Ecosystems & Environment); FAO GSOCseq Technical Report 2020; FAO Recarbonizing
Global Soils, Volumes 1 and 3, 2021.

## Organic matter and water holding capacity

Hudson (1994) demonstrated that available water capacity rises sharply as soil organic matter
increases, with the strongest gains in coarse-textured sandy and sandy loam soils where structural
porosity is otherwise low. Each additional percentage point of organic matter was associated with an
increase of roughly 1.5 to 5 millimetres of plant-available water per metre of soil depth. Clay soils
already hold more water structurally, so the marginal benefit of added organic carbon is smaller there.

## Carbon as the energy base for soil life

Lal (2004) established that soil organic carbon is the primary energy source for microbial and faunal
communities in soil. Microbial biomass rises with SOC up to roughly 3 to 4 percent, after which the
response flattens; additional carbon inputs on already carbon-rich soils yield progressively smaller
increases in biological activity, a saturating rather than linear relationship.

## Cover crops as a practical lever

Poeplau and Don (2015), a meta-analysis spanning 139 paired comparisons, found that introducing cover
crops into a rotation increased soil organic carbon stocks by an average of 0.32 tonnes of carbon per
hectare per year. Legume-based cover crops showed the strongest response because of the additional
nitrogen fixed into the system, which accelerates both plant residue production and its incorporation
into stable soil carbon pools. Gains become measurable from roughly the second growing season onward
and continue accumulating for several years before approaching a new equilibrium.

## Regional practice notes

FAO's Recarbonizing Global Soils volumes document field-scale practices for rebuilding soil carbon,
including mulching, reduced tillage, and organic amendment substitution, each of which raises topsoil
moisture retention and organic carbon over a single to several growing seasons depending on climate and
soil texture.
