# Agroforestry: ecosystem service gains and water trade-offs

Source excerpts: Kuyah et al. 2019 (Agronomy for Sustainable Development); World Agroforestry / CIFOR-ICRAF,
Agroforestry and Water: Trade-offs in Water-Limited Landscapes, 2015; FAO, Advancing Agroforestry on the
Policy Agenda, 2013.

## The upside

Kuyah et al. (2019), a meta-analysis of 53 studies across sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, found that
agroforestry systems increased soil organic carbon by an average of 21 percent relative to monoculture
control plots, alongside measurable increases in canopy cover, pollinator visitation, and habitat
connectivity. These gains typically take 3 to 5 years to become substantial because tree establishment
and canopy development are slow relative to annual cropping changes.

## The trade-off that gets missed

The ICRAF water trade-offs report is explicit about a failure mode that simple "plant more trees"
advice ignores: high-transpiration species, eucalyptus being the most commonly cited example, draw
substantially more groundwater than the crops or grasses they replace. In semi-arid and arid zones
where rainfall does not recharge the water table quickly, observed sites show the local water table
deepening by roughly 0.1 to 1.2 metres over 2 to 5 years following large-scale planting of such species.
This effect is negligible in humid, high-rainfall zones where recharge is fast, and negligible for
low-transpiration native species. Any recommendation to add canopy cover in a water-stressed context
should check the transpiration demand of the specific species proposed, not just the intervention
category.

## Policy framing

FAO's agroforestry policy document frames these systems as delivering runoff reduction and nutrient
retention on top of the carbon and biodiversity gains, through canopy interception and deeper root
uptake of nutrients that would otherwise wash off exposed cropland.
