# Landscape structure, pollinators, and species richness

Source excerpts: IPBES Global Assessment Report on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services, Chapter 5, 2019;
IPBES Land Degradation and Restoration Assessment, 2018; IPBES Pollinators, Pollination and Food
Production Assessment, 2016; Tscharntke et al., Landscape Moderation of Biodiversity Patterns and
Processes, Biological Reviews, 2012; Garibaldi et al., Wild Pollinators Enhance Fruit Set of Crops
Regardless of Honey Bee Abundance, Science, 2013.

## Fragmentation as a primary driver of decline

The IPBES Global Assessment identifies habitat fragmentation as one of the most consistent and
best-evidenced drivers of local species richness decline across terrestrial ecosystems worldwide,
frequently outweighing the effect of habitat loss alone when patches become too small or too isolated
for viable populations to persist.

## Pollinators as a connective indicator

Garibaldi et al. (2013) showed that wild pollinator abundance predicts crop fruit set independently of
managed honeybee presence, and that this abundance responds strongly to the amount of semi-natural
habitat, including woody canopy cover, within typical pollinator foraging range. Tscharntke et al.
(2012) extended this into a landscape-moderation framework: the benefit of any local habitat
intervention depends on the surrounding landscape's fragmentation level. A hedgerow planted in an
already-fragmented, 90+ percent cropland landscape delivers a smaller pollinator benefit than the same
hedgerow planted where some connective habitat already exists, because pollinators still need
continuous forage across the season, not a single flowering patch.

## Soil biota and the wider food web

The IPBES Land Degradation and Restoration Assessment links soil biota health to aboveground food web
structure: healthy microbial and invertebrate soil communities underpin plant community diversity,
which in turn supports higher trophic levels including pollinators and birds.

## Pollution load

The pollinators assessment additionally documents that nutrient runoff and agrochemical load reduce
local biodiversity, with particularly strong effects on aquatic and edge-habitat species that are
sensitive to eutrophication and chemical exposure.
